## What this section is about

This section is about the mathematics of sports betting, and only the mathematics: how odds encode probability, where the bookmaker's margin hides, what expected value actually is, why short-term results prove almost nothing, and what bankroll management can and cannot do.

Who it is for: people who bet and want to see what each bet really costs; people who do not bet but keep hearing about "value" and want the claim unpacked; and anyone who has ever been shown a tipster's screenshot and felt the urge to check the arithmetic. What it is not: tips, predictions, "sure things", or a system for beating the bookmaker. The honest starting point of every article here is that, after the margin, the average bet has negative expected value by construction. A bookmaker is a business, and the price list is where the business lives.

## The concepts everything else is built on

### Odds are prices, and prices imply probabilities

Decimal 2.50, fractional 6/4 and American +150 are the same number in three notations. Invert a decimal price and you get the implied probability: 1 / 2.50 = 40%. Every piece of betting math starts with this conversion, and most betting confusion starts with skipping it.

### Bookmaker margin — the overround

Add up the implied probabilities of all outcomes at any bookmaker and you get more than 100% — on a typical football match line, somewhere around 102–108%. That excess is the margin: every outcome is sold slightly dearer than its fair probability. Margin is why "picking more winners than losers" is not the bar; the bar is beating the price, which is considerably higher.

### Expected value

EV is the sum, over all outcomes, of probability times profit — the long-run average of a bet repeated forever. After the margin, a bet placed at the bookmaker's own implied probabilities has negative EV automatically. Positive EV requires your probability estimate to be systematically better than a market that aggregates enormous amounts of money and information — an extraordinary claim that deserves the same skepticism you would apply to anyone else making it.

### Variance and sample size

A coin weighted 45/55 against you will still hand you winning weeks, and a month at a 55% hit rate proves close to nothing. Separating skill from noise takes samples of thousands of bets, which is why almost every "profitable record" you will ever be shown is statistically indistinguishable from luck. Variance is also why losing streaks feel personal while being mathematically ordinary.

### The closing line

The odds available just before an event starts are the market's most informed estimate, sharpened by all the money that has arrived. Consistently beating the closing line is the least gameable indicator that a bettor's estimates carry real information; showing a profit while consistently failing to beat it is the signature of a lucky sample. It is a diagnostic, not a strategy.

### Bankroll management

Staking discipline — flat stakes, fixed percentages, fractional Kelly — controls how fast variance can ruin you; it does not change the sign of your EV. No staking sequence turns a negative-EV bet positive: martingale and its relatives merely gather all the losses into one rare, catastrophic bet. Bankroll management is a brake, not an engine.
